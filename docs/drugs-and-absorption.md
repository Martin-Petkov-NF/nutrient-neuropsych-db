# Medications, absorption, and the claims that do not hold

19 September 2026. This covers the risk-factor column of the table, and the wider set of questions raised alongside it: acid blockers, antacids, antibiotics, antiperistaltics, GLP-1 drugs, low stomach acid, transdermal supplements, and the things said to block iron absorption.

**One rule runs through all of it.** Three claims get treated as one in almost every drug-and-nutrient list in circulation, and separating them is the whole value of this document.

1. The drug changes the biomarker.
2. The drug causes deficiency by a recognized clinical definition.
3. The deficiency the drug causes produces clinical or neuropsychiatric consequences in real patients.

Almost everything on these lists stops at 1. A handful reach 2. Very little reaches 3.

## The short answer, by drug

| Drug or class | Nutrient | Where the evidence stops |
|---|---|---|
| Proton pump inhibitors | Magnesium | **Reaches 3**, and is the only one here that closes the chain. Rare |
| Isoniazid | Vitamin B6 | **Reaches 3.** Peripheral neuropathy, and pyridoxine prevents it |
| Metformin | B12 | **Reaches 2, and 3 for nerve only.** Nothing has ever tested mood or cognition |
| Nitrous oxide | B12 | **Reaches 3.** Subacute combined degeneration |
| Certain cephalosporins | Vitamin K | **Reaches 3**, but only in patients whose intake is already low. A bleeding risk, not a neuropsychiatric one |
| Trimethoprim | Folate | **Reaches 1**, confirmed by trial. Clinical deficiency only in predisposed groups |
| Proton pump inhibitors | B12, iron | **Reaches 1**, and even that is contested |
| H2 blockers | B12, iron | **Reaches 1**, weakly |
| Anticonvulsants | Folate | **Reaches 1.** Repletion changed nothing |
| GLP-1 receptor agonists | Anything | **Does not reach 1.** No controlled biomarker data exist |
| Antiperistaltics, opioids | Anything | **Does not reach 1**, and the measured direction runs the other way |
| Antibiotics generally | B vitamins | **Does not reach 1.** No controlled human study exists |
| Antacids | B12, magnesium | **Does not reach 1.** No evidence base at ordinary use |

## Proton pump inhibitors and magnesium, the one real syndrome

Worth teaching properly, because it is the only place in this whole subject where the chain from drug to biomarker to symptom closes, and it closes on the strongest possible evidence, which is rechallenge.

**What it looks like.** Tetany, seizures, tremor, carpopedal spasm, atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, prolonged QT. Serum magnesium is profoundly low, with secondary hypocalcemia, hypokalemia and a relatively low parathyroid hormone. Urinary magnesium and calcium excretion are near zero, which localizes the defect to intestinal absorption rather than renal wasting.

**The evidence that makes it causal.** A systematic review of published cases found recovery a median of 4 days after stopping and recurrence within 4 days on restarting (Hess 2012, PMID 22762246). The Food and Drug Administration reported a median of about a week to normalization and two weeks to recurrence, with both positive dechallenge and positive rechallenge across cases.

**How common.** Rare, and the course should say so in the same breath. Median onset is 5.5 years of use, range 14 days to 13 years. Against roughly 21 million annual US users there were about 30 published cases as of 2013. The best absolute estimate is **one excess hospitalization with hypomagnesemia per 76,591 outpatients treated for 90 days** (Zipursky 2014, PMID 25268962). The American Gastroenterological Association reads the rarity against the exposure and concludes these may represent an idiosyncratic reaction (PMID 28257716).

**The population-level signal is much smaller than the case reports suggest.** Pooled odds of hypomagnesemia about 1.71, with a dose gradient, and heavily modified by diuretic use. Two studies found the effect only in diuretic users.

**The guidance conflicts, and the course should say which to follow.** The Food and Drug Administration in 2011 advised considering a magnesium level before starting prolonged treatment and periodically after, particularly alongside digoxin or diuretics. The American Gastroenterological Association in 2017 advised the opposite: long-term users should not routinely screen or monitor bone density, creatinine, magnesium or B12. The narrowest reading satisfying both is to check magnesium in the specified risk groups and not to screen unselected long-term users for anything.

**H2 blockers are the recommended substitute** when this happens. Switching prevented recurrence, and two studies used H2 blockers as a negative control and found no association.

## Proton pump inhibitors and B12, the claim that is everywhere and demonstrated nowhere

**Absorption changes, and quickly.** Two weeks of omeprazole cut absorption of protein-bound B12 from 3.2% to 0.9% at 20 mg and from 3.4% to 0.4% at 40 mg (PMID 8273984). This is real and is the mechanistic basis of the whole claim. It measures absorption of a test dose, not body stores.

**The status evidence contradicts itself.** A case-control study of 26,000 cases found two years or more of use gave odds of 1.65 with a dose gradient (Lam 2013, PMID 24327038). Against that: a cross-sectional study using patients' own non-using partners as controls, which matches household diet, found no difference in B12, homocysteine or cell size (PMID 18194503). Five and twelve years of randomized continuous therapy produced no clinically relevant change in B12, folate or homocysteine (PMID 25858519). The most recent meta-analysis pooled 25 studies to an odds ratio of 1.42 and its own authors wrote that this was too low to imply an association clearly (PMID 37060552).

**No clinical consequence has been shown.** This is the important sentence. No study demonstrates neuropathy, cognitive change, delirium or megaloblastic anemia attributable to proton pump inhibitor associated B12 change. In the Zollinger-Ellison cohort, B12 fell 30% over five years in achlorhydric patients and **no hematologic parameter changed** (PMID 9626024).

**Two confounders are unaddressed in all of it.** Atrophic gastritis and Helicobacter pylori cause both dyspepsia, which leads to prescribing, and B12 malabsorption. And patients on chronic therapy get more blood tests, so an incident diagnosis code is partly a detection artifact.

The drug label matches this evidence better than the folklore does. It prompts considering the diagnosis if symptoms consistent with deficiency appear. It does not recommend monitoring.

## Calcium, and a distinction that resolves an apparent contradiction

Studies disagree about whether acid suppression impairs calcium absorption, and the disagreement resolves cleanly once you look at what was given and how.

Absorption of **calcium carbonate taken fasting** is badly impaired without stomach acid: 4.2% against 45.2% for calcium citrate in achlorhydric patients (PMID 4000241). Absorption of **calcium from food, or from a soluble salt**, is not affected. Two controlled studies using food-based calcium found no change (PMID 8568113, PMID 20578215), and in the 1985 study absorption normalized completely when the same carbonate was taken with breakfast.

So the practical point for a patient on acid suppression is about how they take a calcium supplement, not about whether they can absorb calcium.

On fractures, the observational signal is moderately consistent at about 1.30 pooled, but hip fracture was exactly 1.00 in the Women's Health Initiative, null in a three-year randomized trial of 17,598 people, absent below five to seven years of use, confined to smokers in one cohort and to people with another risk factor in another, and there is no bone density mechanism to support it. The Food and Drug Administration added an over-the-counter fracture warning in May 2010 and removed it ten months later.

## Metformin and B12, the best-established pair

Randomized, 4.3 years: B12 fell 19%, with a number needed to harm of 13.8 (PMID 20488910). The Diabetes Prevention Program follow-up found odds of 1.13 per year of use across 13 years (PMID 26900641). This one genuinely reaches clinical definition.

For consequences, be precise. Higher neuropathy prevalence has been reported in metformin users with low B12, but as a within-arm association in a secondary analysis, not a randomized result. **No study has ever tested whether metformin-associated low B12 affects mood or cognition.** For a course about psychiatric misdiagnosis, that absence is the finding.

Metformin should not appear on a folate risk list. Its effect on folate was 5% and lost significance after adjustment for body mass index and smoking.

## Antibiotics, where most of the folklore lives

**The general claim fails.** No controlled human study shows measurable B vitamin change after a standard antibiotic course in adults. The supporting literature is genome annotation and animal work, not measurement of human vitamin status.

**It fails on anatomy for B12 specifically.** B12 absorption is intrinsic-factor mediated and happens in the ileum, upstream of the colon. B12 made by colonic bacteria cannot be absorbed at all. Patients with total colectomy had normal B12 analogue levels while those with diseased or resected ileum had low ones.

**Folate is the exception that proves the rule.** The human colon does absorb folate, at about 46% apparent absorption when delivered directly. Even so, nobody has shown that a course of antibiotics moves human folate status.

**Three antibiotic effects are real and specific.**

*Isoniazid and B6* is the strongest drug, nutrient and neuropsychiatric chain in this entire document. Pyridoxal phosphate fell in nearly every patient within seven days of starting therapy, and the fall happened despite improvement in inflammatory markers, which rules out the obvious confounder. The 2016 American Thoracic Society, Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and Infectious Diseases Society of America guideline directs 25 to 50 mg of pyridoxine daily for everyone at risk of neuropathy, rising to 100 mg for those who have it.

*Certain cephalosporins and vitamin K* is real but narrower than usually told. Drugs carrying particular side chains inhibit the vitamin K cycle in a weak warfarin-like way. It is clinically silent in patients eating normally and appears within four days in patients on parenteral nutrition. It is a bleeding risk, not a neuropsychiatric one, and it is not a property of broad-spectrum antibiotics as a class.

*Trimethoprim and folate* is confirmed by a randomized placebo-controlled trial in 60 healthy men: serum folate fell about 2 nmol/L over seven days (PMID 26566149). One caution for anyone reading the older literature. Two studies found that the apparent folate fall on these drugs was partly an artifact of the microbiological assay, which the drug interferes with, and disappeared using a different method. Any pre-1980 citation resting on that assay is unreliable.

**Linezolid should not be listed as a B vitamin depleter.** Its neuropathy is real and its label carries a warning, but the label contains no nutrient statement, no trial has tested whether pyridoxine prevents it, and the leading mechanism is mitochondrial. Its serotonin toxicity is a drug interaction, since linezolid is a reversible monoamine oxidase inhibitor, and has nothing to do with nutrition.

## GLP-1 drugs, where the honest answer is that nobody has measured it

**No controlled micronutrient biomarker data exist as of September 2026.** A post hoc analysis of the four phase 3 tirzepatide trials, 4,726 people, states that micronutrient biomarkers were not systematically measured under the protocols. The only nutrition-related measures collected were albumin and lymphocyte count, and the authors note neither is recommended as a nutritional marker any more.

What those trials do show, from investigator-reported adverse events: vitamin deficiency in 0.99% on tirzepatide against 1.07% on placebo. **Numerically lower on the drug.**

**The figure now circulating comes from claims data and shrinks under matching.** A retrospective analysis of 461,382 adults reported any nutritional deficiency in 22.4% within 12 months and vitamin D deficiency in 13.6%. In the matched comparison inside the same paper, 12-month figures were 18.6% against 16.5% for any deficiency and 10.9% against 9.2% for vitamin D. Absolute differences of one to two percentage points. Iron deficiency anemia was not raised at all. Deficiencies were more likely to be recorded in patients who had seen a dietitian, which is a detection effect. These are billing codes, not biomarkers, and most of the authors worked for a supplement manufacturer.

**The one signal worth watching is thiamine.** Fifteen cases of Wernicke encephalopathy associated with these drugs have been identified, nearly all reported in 2023 and 2024, and 13 of 15 had severe vomiting, appetite loss or malnutrition. Seven of eleven followed up had lasting neurological damage. That is a spontaneous reporting signal subject to notoriety bias, and protracted vomiting is a route to thiamine deficiency whatever causes it. It is worth knowing and it is not yet a rate.

**None of the four product labels contains any nutrient warning.** The labels do address delayed gastric emptying, and where they do, the concern is absorption of other drugs, not nutrients.

## Reduced motility, where the inference runs backwards

The requester's list includes antiperistaltics such as dicyclomine and notes that GLP-1 drugs slow motility. The inference that slower transit causes malabsorption has never been tested in humans, and the direct measurements that exist point the other way.

In short bowel patients, a population with severely compromised absorption, codeine and loperamide produced clinically unimportant changes in nutrient absorption. Octreotide slowed transit and **increased** water, sodium and calorie absorption. A fibre supplement slowed transit and decreased absorption. Transit time and absorption did not move together at all (PMID 2491467).

Loperamide is standard therapy in short bowel syndrome precisely because slowing transit improves nutrient retention. Reviews of opioid-induced bowel dysfunction describe decreased propulsion **and increased fluid absorption**.

The dicyclomine and oxybutynin labels contain no mention of vitamin, nutrient, malabsorption or deficiency.

**One real bridge exists and it is incomplete.** Bacterial overgrowth does cause B12 malabsorption, by competition with intrinsic factor, and slowed motility does predispose to overgrowth. But no study has enrolled patients on these drugs, confirmed overgrowth, and measured B12 as an outcome. The chain is assembled from two verified segments and one unmeasured join. The gastroenterology practice update also notes that even in confirmed overgrowth, B12 deficiency is the less common finding, while **raised** folate is the more common one.

**A separate, real effect worth listing correctly.** Anticholinergic burden is associated with worsening nutritional status in two cohorts of older adults. The route is reduced intake through dry mouth, not malabsorption, and the outcome measures were composite nutrition scores, not micronutrient levels. If anticholinergics are listed, list them there.

**One exclusion the course must make explicitly.** Anticholinergic burden is associated with dementia, with a clear dose-response across two large studies. Neither measured any nutrient and neither proposes a nutritional mechanism. The likely explanation is direct central muscarinic blockade. Crediting that association to nutrition would give the nutrition table a borrowed strength it has not earned, and it is the single most damaging error available in this area.

## Low stomach acid, where the premise is half right and half wrong

This deserves the most care of anything here, because it is the wider brief's central mechanism and because the popular version of it is wrong in a specific, checkable way.

**Aging does not cause low stomach acid.** Three direct-measurement studies agree, and none of them supports the received wisdom.

Measuring basal gastric pH in 248 independently living people aged 65 and over, **84% had acidic stomach contents**, and on retest the population estimate was 67% consistently acid-secreting, 22% intermittent, and **11% consistently hyposecreting** (Hurwitz 1997, PMID 9272898). Measuring acid output directly in 206 people aged 18 to 98, stimulated output was about 30% lower in the old, but **after adjusting for stomach histology and Helicobacter pylori infection, age had no independent effect at all** (Feldman 1996, PMID 8612992). A third study found acid secretion was actually **higher** in older subjects, with age an independent positive predictor and Helicobacter pylori an independent negative one (PMID 1889722).

So the cause is not age. The cause is a disease, atrophic gastritis, usually driven by Helicobacter pylori, which is present in a minority and has a serologic marker. That distinction is the whole difference between treating everyone over 50 and identifying the people who actually have it.

**Where the 30% figure comes from, and what it actually measured.** The number traces to a 1998 Institute of Medicine report stating that 10% to 30% of people older than 50 are estimated to have **atrophic gastritis with low acid secretion**, and recommending that people over 50 get their B12 from fortified food or a supplement. The underlying Boston study found atrophic gastritis in 31.5% of 359 people aged 60 to 99, measured by a serum pepsinogen ratio (Krasinski 1986, PMID 3771980).

That study measured a blood marker of stomach lining atrophy. **It did not measure stomach acid and nobody in it was intubated.** The figure is about a serologic finding in a cohort that included institutionalized people and predates the decline in Helicobacter pylori prevalence, so it probably overstates the US position today.

One citation correction worth passing on, because it will otherwise fail a check: that study is a Boston cohort from the Tufts nutrition research center, not a Framingham study. The Framingham cobalamin work is a different paper.

**What is genuinely established, and it is the strongest argument in this whole area.** Food-cobalamin malabsorption is real, and it is the inability to release B12 from the food proteins it is bound to. It is invisible to the classic absorption test, because that test uses free B12. In a French series it accounted for more than 60% of all cobalamin deficiency in older patients, against 15% to 20% for pernicious anemia.

**And crystalline B12 bypasses it completely.** In people with atrophic gastritis, crystalline B12 was absorbed just as well as in controls, while protein-bound B12 absorption was cut to about a third (PMID 1889697). High-dose oral B12 corrected established deficiency as well as injections did, with prompt and indistinguishable correction of both blood and neurological abnormalities (PMID 9694707).

The dose matters and it is far above the recommended intake. A randomized dose-finding study in older people found the dose achieving most of the achievable reduction in methylmalonic acid was **647 to 1,032 micrograms a day, more than 200 times the recommended intake** (PMID 15911731). This is what the standard advice for adults over 50 already encodes, and it is the one place where a concern about impaired digestion leads somewhere real and actionable.

**Iron and autoimmune gastritis, a detail worth having.** In autoimmune gastritis specifically, iron deficiency appears decades before B12 deficiency. Patients presenting with iron deficiency anemia were 21 years younger than those presenting with a macrocytic picture, and mostly women. Pernicious anemia is not a disease of old age. It is the late stage of a process that starts much earlier and first shows up as iron deficiency. Note that in the general elderly cohort with age-related atrophic gastritis, iron indices did **not** differ, so these are two different diseases sharing a histologic name.

For calcium, the effect of low acid is confined to fasting calcium carbonate, as above.

## Diuretics

Included because they are among the most commonly prescribed drugs in the population this course concerns, and because one of them has a genuine neuropsychiatric consequence that is not a nutrient deficiency at all.

**Thiazides and low sodium is the real finding, and it is the strongest drug-to-neuropsychiatric link in this document.** Thiazide exposure carried a hazard ratio of 4.95 for low sodium in a population cohort of 13,325 adults (PMID 23602191). Those who present are typically about 75 years old and 79% women. **The mean time from starting the drug to presentation is 19 days**, which means the customary single check at one or two weeks is likely to miss it (PMID 25139696).

The consequences are behavioral and they reverse. In patients with mild chronic low sodium, unsteadiness and attention were measurably impaired, and both improved when the sodium was corrected, with the authors comparing the size of the effect to alcohol. Falls and fractures were substantially more common, replicated across three datasets. Be careful with the effect sizes, since one confidence interval spans two orders of magnitude, but the direction is credible and replicated.

This belongs in the course precisely because it looks like a psychiatric or cognitive problem, is caused by a drug, and is fixed by stopping the drug. It is not a nutrient deficiency.

**Loop diuretics and thiamine is much weaker than it looks.** The prevalence range in the literature runs from 3% to 98%, and **the spread is an assay artifact rather than a population difference**. Functional activation assays give the 90%-plus figures. Direct measurement of thiamine diphosphate by chromatography gives under 12% in comparable outpatients, and no difference at all in two cohorts. One study showed that the fraction which falls is not the storage form, and concluded this is altered metabolism rather than deficiency.

The trial evidence went the same way as it accumulated. Two early small trials suggested a benefit on heart function, and two meta-analyses built on them were positive. Once a six-month randomized trial was added in which ejection fraction was **significantly higher in the placebo group** despite confirmed biochemical repletion, the pooled effect disappeared. The two most recent meta-analyses are null on ejection fraction, symptoms, walk distance and blood markers. No trial has shown an effect on death or hospitalization, and no heart failure guideline recommends thiamine screening or supplementation.

Diuretic-associated Wernicke encephalopathy is asserted rather than demonstrated. The case reports all have a sufficient alternative cause present, such as vomiting, poor intake, or thiamine-free parenteral nutrition. Diuretic-associated cardiac beriberi sits on firmer ground, with two cases showing dramatic reversal within hours of treatment.

**Thiazides and calcium break the pattern, and clinicians reasoning from "diuretics deplete" will get this one backwards.** Thiazides **reduce** urinary calcium excretion. Observational data associate them with 24% lower hip fracture risk. The drug label instructs stopping them before parathyroid testing, because they can unmask primary hyperparathyroidism.

**Magnesium and potassium.** Thiazides raise urinary magnesium, serum magnesium usually stays in range, and no randomized trial shows that correcting it changes any patient-level outcome. The one solid clinical rule is that refractory low potassium cannot be corrected without correcting magnesium first. Low potassium is well established and dose related, and the claim that it mediates the diabetes seen with these drugs rests on a post hoc analysis of a single trial.

**Potassium-sparing drugs run the other way, and the harm is documented at population scale.** After a landmark trial, spironolactone prescribing in Ontario rose fourfold, hyperkalemia hospitalizations rose from 2.4 to 11.0 per thousand, and associated deaths rose from 0.3 to 2.0 per thousand, with no fall in heart failure readmission or death. Correcting one electrolyte problem created a worse one.

## Transdermal and topical supplements: the evidence does not support them

Topical vitamin D, B complex and magnesium are promoted for people with impaired digestion or no stomach acid. This is the clearest negative here and it deserves a careful answer rather than a dismissal, because the people most drawn to it include pharmacists who will check the sources.

**The two human comparative studies both found patches inferior to oral.** In patients after gastric bypass, 82% of the patch group had at least one deficiency against 41% of the oral group. In runners, oral iron beat patches on ferritin.

**Magnesium** has one small pilot study whose whole-group result was null.

**Vitamin D** has a single unreplicated trial from one group, and none of the papers citing it is a human replication.

**The regulatory position closes it.** A dietary supplement is by definition intended for ingestion, so a topical product cannot legally be one. A transdermal system delivering a substance systemically is a drug and requires a new drug application. No vitamin or mineral has been through that pathway.

The honest framing for the course: if transdermal delivery of these nutrients worked, there would be pharmacokinetic studies showing blood levels rising, and there would be an approved product. Neither exists.

## Dairy, tea, coffee and calcium against iron

The requester is right that these inhibit iron absorption in single-meal studies, and the effect is large. Calcium carbonate taken with a meal cut non-heme iron absorption from 15.8% to 4.7%.

It is unlikely to matter over time, which is the question that counts. **Six months of 1,200 mg daily calcium supplementation produced no change in hemoglobin, hematocrit, zinc protoporphyrin or plasma ferritin in iron-replete adults** (PMID 9665102).

This gap between single-meal absorption studies and long-term status studies is the single most useful methodological point in this whole area, and it generalizes. Absorption from one meal is measured over hours. Iron status reflects months. Bodies adapt, and up-regulated absorption compensates for a good deal of short-term inhibition.

The practical advice that survives is modest and worth keeping: someone treating diagnosed iron deficiency should take the supplement away from calcium, tea and coffee. Someone who is iron-replete does not need to rearrange their diet around it.

One substantive position in the plant-based nutrition literature runs against the usual framing and is worth engaging with rather than assuming past: that the lower iron stores typically seen in vegetarians are a benefit rather than a shortfall, with menstruating women the named exception. Coffee and non-herbal tea are well documented as absorption inhibitors on the same body of work.

## Malabsorptive disease, and two syndromes that are not what they look like

Celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease appear on every drug-and-nutrient list as malabsorption risks. Both are real risks. But the two conditions in which they produce neurological disease turn out **not** to be nutrient-mediated, and a course teaching differential diagnosis needs to know that before it uses either as an example.

**Gluten ataxia is an immune syndrome, not a deficiency.** Five independent lines say so. Only about a quarter of patients have any intestinal damage, so there is no malabsorptive lesion in three quarters of cases. The antibody involved targets a transglutaminase found in nerve tissue, and the response occurs independently of gut involvement. Antibody deposits are found in the cerebellum, pons and medulla. The neuropathology is inflammatory, with cell loss accompanied by T-lymphocyte infiltration. And injecting the antibody alone reproduces ataxia in mice with no dietary manipulation at all.

Most decisively, recovery on a gluten-free diet tracks **antibody clearance, not nutrient repletion**: brain spectroscopy improved in 62 of 63 patients who cleared antibodies, in 9 of 35 who stayed on the diet but remained antibody positive, and in 1 of 19 not on the diet. **Do not use gluten ataxia as an example of a nutrient deficiency causing neuropsychiatric symptoms.** It is the opposite.

**Celiac-associated neuropathy is also not nutrient-mediated, and this was tested directly.** A nationwide cohort of 28,232 people with biopsy-verified celiac disease found a hazard ratio of 2.5 for neuropathy (PMID 25962148). The authors adjusted for vitamin deficiencies among other covariates and report that doing so **had only a marginal effect on the risk estimate**. The association survives removal of the nutritional explanation.

**What is genuinely nutrient-mediated in celiac disease is copper**, and it is rare. Copper deficiency myelopathy is clinically indistinguishable from the B12 syndrome, the blood abnormalities resolve on repletion, and the neurological damage may not.

**Two practical findings for the course.**

In inflammatory bowel disease, **the nutrient everyone checks is the one least often abnormal.** In hospitalized patients, B12 was tested in 97% and was low in 9%, while B6 was low in 40% and zinc in 33% of the far smaller numbers tested for them.

And **B12 deficiency in unoperated Crohn disease or in ulcerative colitis is not established.** It is established after ileal resection over 20 cm. A meta-analysis of 12 studies found no significant difference in B12 between patients and controls. The single cohorts reporting 41% and 19% are not representative, and none of the studies used a confirmatory functional marker.

**One positive result worth knowing, with its caveat.** High-dose thiamine, 600 to 1,800 mg daily, significantly reduced chronic fatigue in a double-blind crossover trial of 40 patients with quiescent inflammatory bowel disease. Follow-up work from the same group found the effect was **not** explained by correcting a thiamine deficiency. It is a pharmacologic dose effect, so it does not belong in a deficiency table without that caveat.

## Psychiatric drugs themselves

Most relevant to this audience and thinner than expected.

Lithium's real effect is hypercalcemia through hyperparathyroidism, at roughly 4% against 0.5%, which is not nutrient depletion. Enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants affect vitamin D and fracture risk, and lower folate, but repleting folate in that population produced no benefit on intelligence, behavior, mental health or personality. Valproate affects carnitine. **No controlled human evidence was found that selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors deplete any micronutrient.** The strongest clinical signal for that class is hyponatremia, which is an electrolyte effect and not a nutritional one.
