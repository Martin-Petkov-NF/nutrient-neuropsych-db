# Nutrient deficiencies that can produce neuropsychiatric symptoms

Draft, 19 September 2026. Prepared with AI assistance. Every citation below was checked against the PubMed record. **No clinician has yet signed this off, so treat it as a sourced literature summary and not as clinical guidance.** See [DISCLAIMER.md](../DISCLAIMER.md).

Built to a brief asking which nutrient deficiencies can produce neuropsychiatric symptoms, at what prevalence, detected by which tests. Companion documents: [methods.md](methods.md) for the evidence bar, [corrections.md](corrections.md) for figures that did not survive checking, and [drugs-and-absorption.md](drugs-and-absorption.md) for the medication risk factors.

## How to read this table

**Subclinical and clinical are kept apart throughout**, in separate columns with separate references, because the argument depends on the distinction. Frank deficiencies are less frequent, more apparent and less likely to be overlooked. The question the course is asking is whether a common subclinical state contributes to psychiatric misdiagnosis.

**Every prevalence figure states its biomarker, its cutoff and its data years.** A figure without those three is not usable and is not here. Where the cutoff is contested, the table reports the disagreement rather than choosing.

**Intake inadequacy and biochemical deficiency are labeled separately and never substituted for one another.** This is the most common error in the material on this subject. A population falling below an estimated average requirement is an arithmetic property of how that requirement is defined. It is not a measure of anyone's nutrient status and it implies nothing about symptoms.

**The evidence grade is per claim, not per nutrient.** A nutrient can have strong evidence for its clinical presentation and no usable evidence for a subclinical one. Most do.

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Strong | Replicated interventional evidence, or a deficiency syndrome not in dispute |
| Moderate | Consistent observational evidence with a defensible case for direction, or one good trial |
| Weak | Cross-sectional only, small, or confounded in a way the source does not resolve |
| Insufficient | The literature was searched and no adequate source was found. Not the same as evidence of absence |

**No claim in this table rests on a neurotransmitter mechanism.** At the brief's instruction, proposed pathways through dopamine, serotonin, glutamate or GABA are not treated as evidence. Where the popular case for a nutrient consists only of such a pathway, the table says so and grades the claim accordingly.

## The source of record for prevalence

The [CDC National Report on Biochemical Indicators of Diet and Nutrition](https://www.cdc.gov/nutrition-report/index.html), 2026 edition, released 24 June 2026, covering NHANES biomarker data from 1999 through August 2023 across 131 indicators, stratified by dietary supplement use for the first time, and reported by age, sex, and race and Hispanic origin.

This matters for a practical reason. Almost every figure circulating in the material behind this request predates that edition, several by more than a decade, and some by more than twenty years.

## Table

Set out as one block per nutrient rather than one wide grid. The brief's columns are all here and in its order. A grid with ten columns is unreadable on paper and unusable on a phone, and the spreadsheet version carries the same fields for anyone who wants to sort.

One warning that applies before any row is read. **NHANES stopped measuring serum B12 after the 2013 to 2014 cycle and has never measured holotranscobalamin.** Every current US prevalence figure for B12 is at least twelve years old. Folate is the only one of the two under continuous surveillance.

### Vitamin B12 (cobalamin)

**Subclinical prevalence, US adults.** There is no consensus cutoff, and that is the dominant fact rather than a quibble. In the same NHANES sample, prevalence moves between 3% and 26% on cutoff choice alone.

| Definition | Prevalence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Serum B12 below 221 pmol/L, adults 20 and over | 7.3% in 1999 to 2000, rising to 10.7% in 2013 to 2014, significant linear trend | Pfeiffer 2026, [PMID 42163863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163863/), NHANES |
| Serum B12 148 to 222 pmol/L | 13% | Mineva 2021, [PMID 33963731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33963731/), NHANES 1999 to 2004 |
| Plasma methylmalonic acid above 271 nmol/L | 6.5% rising to 9.4% over the same period | Pfeiffer 2026, [PMID 42163863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163863/) |
| Plasma methylmalonic acid above 376 nmol/L | 3% to 4%, no significant change | Pfeiffer 2026, [PMID 42163863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163863/) |
| Combined indicator, low or transitional | 2.7% | Mineva 2021, [PMID 33963731](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33963731/) |
| Marginal without anemia, adults over 50 | 20.7% post-fortification | Qi 2014, [PMID 24306216](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24306216/) |

**Clinical prevalence.** Serum B12 below 148 pmol/L: 1.0% in 1999 to 2000, 1.8% in 2013 to 2014, no significant trend (Pfeiffer 2026, [PMID 42163863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163863/)). Low B12 together with raised methylmalonic acid, which is the defensible definition of clear deficiency: about 1% of adults, with 92% adequate (Bailey 2011, [PMID 21677051](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21677051/)). Undiagnosed untreated pernicious anemia in adults 60 and over: 1.9%, and 2.7% in women against 1.4% in men (Carmel 1996, [PMID 8638997](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8638997/)).

**By age and sex.** Deficiency rises with age. Reference intervals for serum B12 widen markedly in older adults, and methylmalonic acid is age-dependent even in replete people with normal kidney function, so a fixed cutoff over-diagnoses the old (Mineva 2019, [PMID 31127807](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31127807/)).

On sex the honest finding runs against the expected direction, and the course needs to know it before the table is used. Women had **better** functional B12 status than men on the combined indicator. The group with low serum B12 but normal methylmalonic acid, about 2% of adults, was disproportionately younger women, and the NHANES authors say this may have falsely reflected low B12 (Bailey 2011, [PMID 21677051](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21677051/)). The demographic most likely to be prescribed psychiatric drugs is also the one most likely to produce a low serum B12 that is not functional deficiency. Running the other way, pernicious anemia is about twice as common in older women as in older men.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical: attributed, not demonstrated.** Evidence grade **insufficient**. Every adequately designed trial is null.

- Randomized, 140 people selected for raised methylmalonic acid: treatment normalized the biochemistry and changed nothing clinical (Hvas 2001, [PMID 11468228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11468228/)). The same cohort, reported separately, found no effect on cognitive function or depression.
- Randomized, 195 people aged 70 and over with mild deficiency, full neuropsychological battery: no benefit, and memory improved more in the placebo arm (Eussen 2006, [PMID 16895884](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16895884/)).
- Meta-analysis, 11 trials, 22,000 participants: B vitamins cut homocysteine by 26% to 28% and produced no effect on any cognitive domain (Clarke 2014, [PMID 24965307](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24965307/)).
- Meta-analysis, 11 randomized trials of folate or B12 in depression: no effect (Almeida 2015, [PMID 25644193](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25644193/)).

The observational findings that generate the attribution are weak. The most cited, a 107-person cohort linking low B12 to brain volume loss, is internally inconsistent: methylmalonic acid and homocysteine were not associated, which is the opposite of what a functional B12 explanation predicts (Vogiatzoglou 2008, [PMID 18779510](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18779510/)).

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical: strong, and strong for an unfashionable reason.** Evidence grade **strong**. The pre-SSRI-era case series recorded treatment response, which is what carries the causal weight.

- 141 consecutive patients with neuropsychiatric abnormality from cobalamin deficiency. **40 of them, 28%, had no anemia and no macrocytosis.** Paresthesia, sensory loss, ataxia, dementia and psychiatric disorders. Serum B12 was in the normal range in 2 and in the grey zone in 16 more, so a normal B12 did not exclude it. All 39 evaluable patients improved on treatment (Lindenbaum 1988, [PMID 3374544](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3374544/)).
- 153 episodes over 17 years, including dementia, psychoses and mood disturbance. Hematocrit was normal in 27% and mean cell volume normal in 23%. All 121 evaluable episodes responded, with complete recovery in 47%. **Severity before treatment tracked how long symptoms had gone undiagnosed**, which is the most actionable finding in this table for a deprescribing course (Healton 1991, [PMID 1648656](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1648656/)).

The design caveat must travel with these. They are uncontrolled series in patients referred with neurological disease. They establish that clinical deficiency produces reversible neuropsychiatric disease. They do not estimate how often a psychiatric presentation in an unselected population is caused by it.

**Tests, subclinical.** Serum B12 first line, but methylmalonic acid modelling leaves an intermediate band containing **33% of US adults whose status is uninterpretable** (Bailey 2013, [PMID 23803883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23803883/)). Methylmalonic acid is the best single functional marker, raised by impaired kidney function, by age, and by fasting. Holotranscobalamin is reported to outperform serum B12, but the comparison uses methylmalonic acid as the reference standard, which is partly circular, and NHANES has never measured it, so **no US population reference data exist**. Homocysteine is cheap and not specific, rising with folate and B6 deficiency, renal impairment and hypothyroidism.

The operational rule worth teaching: **do not diagnose subclinical deficiency unless at least two tests are abnormal.** Sensitivity is high across all the markers, specificity is poor, there is no gold standard, and cutpoints move (Carmel 2013, [PMID 23416723](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23416723/)).

**Tests, clinical.** Serum B12 below 148 pmol/L, with blood count and film, a confirmatory functional marker, intrinsic factor antibody, and a malabsorption workup. Teach the two exceptions above: more than a quarter of neuropsychiatric cases have neither anemia nor macrocytosis, and a normal serum B12 does not exclude clinical deficiency in a compatible syndrome.

**Risk factors.**

| Factor | Effect on the biomarker | Shown to produce clinical consequences? |
|---|---|---|
| Metformin | Randomized, 4.3 years: B12 down 19%. Number needed to harm 13.8 (de Jager 2010, [PMID 20488910](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488910/)). Higher neuropathy prevalence with low B12, but as a within-arm association, not a randomized result. **For mood or cognition, no study has ever tested it** |

No for the marginal band |

### Folate (vitamin B9)

**Subclinical prevalence, US adults.** The headline figure in circulation is real and does not mean what it is taken to mean. See the corrections file for the full account.

| Definition | Prevalence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Red cell folate below 748 nmol/L, women 12 to 49 | 23.2% in 2007 to 2010, falling to 18.6% in 2011 to 2016 | Pfeiffer 2019, [PMID 31504109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504109/) |
| Same, women of reproductive age, longer window | 15.7% in 1999 to 2000, rising to 21.4% in 2021 to 2023 | Pfeiffer 2026, [PMID 42163863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163863/) |
| Same, non-Hispanic Black women | 39.0% falling to 33.8% | Pfeiffer 2019, [PMID 31504109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504109/) |
| **Men of any age, and women 50 and over** | **No data exist.** No insufficiency cutoff has ever been defined for these groups | |

That threshold is a **neural tube defect risk threshold** for women who might become pregnant, derived from Irish and Chinese studies and codified by the World Health Organization. It is not a deficiency threshold, it has no established relationship to any adult outcome, and the paper reporting it contains no discussion of neuropsychiatric outcomes.

A second caution on testing. Assay matters more than anything else here. The older radioassay reads about 29% lower in serum and about 45% lower in whole blood than the reference microbiologic assay. **Cutoffs are assay-specific and do not transfer between laboratories.** A clinician applying the 748 nmol/L figure to a commercial protein-binding result is doing something meaningless.

**Clinical prevalence.** **Under 1%** of persons aged 3 and over, in every NHANES cycle from 1999 to 2016 (Pfeiffer 2019, [PMID 31504109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504109/)). Before fortification it was 5.6% by serum and 7.4% by red cell folate. The CDC's 2026 report declined to estimate it at all, on the grounds that it is known to be under 1% in the population and in subgroups because of mandatory fortification. For a US clinical audience, frank folate deficiency is a finding in alcohol use disorder, malabsorption and antifolate drug exposure. It is not a population phenomenon.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical: the evidence does not support the claim.** Evidence grade **insufficient**, and this is the question answered directly.

The structural defect is not fixable by adding studies. **No trial has ever randomized adults selected for subclinical folate deficiency to repletion and measured neuropsychiatric outcomes.** Every trial in this literature selects on depression, not on folate status.

- The largest augmentation trial, 475 people, 5 mg folic acid added to antidepressants, explicitly excluded folate-deficient and B12-deficient people. Null on every outcome, and it **significantly reduced mental health scores by 3.0%**, meaning the folic acid arm did slightly worse. The authors state the finding undermines guidelines advocating folic acid for depression (Bedson 2014, [PMID 25052890](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25052890/)).
- A Cochrane review of folic acid in 331 people taking anticonvulsants, which is exactly the population with drug-induced low folate, found **no effect on intelligence, behavior, mental health or personality** (Ranganathan 2005, [PMID 15846704](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15846704/)). This is the closest thing in existence to a direct test of the proposition, and it is negative.
- A prospective cohort of 3,503 adults aged 65 and over, followed 7.2 years for incident depression, found associations for B6 and B12 intake and **none for folate** (Skarupski 2010, [PMID 20519557](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20519557/)).

On provenance, that instinct is well supported. The influential positive result comes from two trials reported together: the larger, 148 people at 7.5 mg, was **null**, and the smaller, 75 people at 15 mg, was positive. Both were funded by the manufacturer of the prescription methylfolate product (Papakostas 2012, [PMID 23212058](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23212058/)). A real dose-response effect would ordinarily surface first in the better-powered study. The Cochrane review often cited as supporting folate for depression rests on three trials and 247 people, said itself that the application to people with normal folate was unclear, and **has not been updated in 23 years** (Taylor 2003, [PMID 12804463](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12804463/)).

On reverse causation, which is the other half of the objection. The older inpatient literature measured folate in hospitalized psychiatric populations, where low folate tracks diet, alcohol, smoking, poverty, institutional food and anticonvulsant exposure. The association is real. The direction is not established, and nothing in the trial record supports it running from folate to mood.

**Suggested cell text:** no good evidence. No trial has selected on subclinical folate deficiency, the largest augmentation trial was null and trended negative, and repleting drug-induced low folate produced no mental-health benefit in Cochrane review.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical: thin, old and uncontrolled.** Evidence grade **weak**. A comparative case series of 34 folate-deficient and 50 B12-deficient patients with megaloblastosis found nervous system abnormality in two-thirds of both groups, with **affective disorder the commonest association for folate** as peripheral neuropathy was for B12. Subacute combined degeneration occurred only in the B12 group (Shorvon 1980, [PMID 6253016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6253016/)). There is no established folate-specific myelopathy. Unlike the B12 series, this one has no treatment-response data to lean on, so it establishes co-occurrence in hospitalized patients and not causation.

**Tests.** Red cell folate, not serum. Serum folate moves with a single meal, rises 9.5% with fasting and falls 4.5% with inflammation. **There is no validated second-line functional marker for folate.** Homocysteine responds to folate repletion but equally to B12, B6, renal impairment and hypothyroidism. This is the most important asymmetry with B12, which has methylmalonic acid, and it belongs in the course: for B12 a functional marker adds real information, for folate it does not.

For clinical status, red cell folate below 305 nmol/L with blood count and film, and **B12 measured at the same time**, because folate alone corrects the anemia of B12 deficiency while the neurology progresses.

**Risk factors.**

| Factor | Effect on the biomarker | Shown to produce clinical consequences? |
|---|---|---|
| Anticonvulsants: phenytoin, carbamazepine, phenobarbital | Well established lowering | **No**, and the negative is strong. Cochrane review in 331 people taking anticonvulsants found no effect of repletion on intelligence, behavior, mental health or personality |
| Methotrexate, sulfasalazine, trimethoprim | Antifolate by mechanism. Sulfasalazine competitively inhibits intestinal folate transport, dose-dependently | Folic acid reduces methotrexate gastrointestinal and liver toxicity, established in rheumatoid arthritis. No neuropsychiatric data found |
| Alcohol use disorder | The commonest real-world cause of clinical deficiency in the US | Confounded beyond usefulness as a test of the folate hypothesis, since alcohol independently causes the same symptoms |
| Malabsorption | Causes clinical deficiency | Neuropsychiatric attribution rests on one 1980 case series |
| Metformin | Down 5%, **and the effect lost significance after adjustment for body mass index and smoking** (de Jager 2010, [PMID 20488910](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20488910/)) | No. Effectively null. **Do not list metformin as a folate risk factor.** It belongs on the B12 list |

### Vitamin D

**The cutoff disagreement, and why the usual framing is out of date.** The familiar version is that the Institute of Medicine says 20 ng/mL and the Endocrine Society says 30. That describes 2011. The Endocrine Society's 2024 guideline identifies **no** threshold for sufficiency, insufficiency or deficiency, and advises against routine testing in healthy people without an indication (Demay 2024, [PMID 38828931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38828931/)). The 30 ng/mL target has no current guideline sponsor. The live disagreement is now narrower: the Institute of Medicine keeps population cutoffs for surveillance, the Endocrine Society declines to set clinical ones.

Institute of Medicine bands, which NHANES uses: below 30 nmol/L, at risk of deficiency. 30 to under 50 nmol/L, at risk of inadequacy. 50 to 75 nmol/L, sufficient.

**Subclinical prevalence, US population.**

| Definition | Prevalence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Below 50 nmol/L, standardized assay | 28% in 2001 to 2002, falling to **22%** in 2021 to 2023 | CDC 2026 report |
| At risk of inadequacy, 30 to 49 nmol/L | 18.3% | Herrick 2019, [PMID 31076739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31076739/), NHANES 2011 to 2014 |
| At risk of deficiency, below 30 nmol/L | **5.0%** | Herrick 2019, [PMID 31076739](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31076739/) |
| Above 125 nmol/L, excess | under 1% rising to 8% | CDC 2026 report |

By age, deficiency **peaks at 20 to 39 years** at 7.6%, and is 2.9% in those 60 and over. That is the opposite of the intuition, and it matters here because it overlaps the age band in which psychiatric prescribing is heaviest.

By race and Hispanic origin, deficiency ran from 2.1% in non-Hispanic White people to 17.5% in non-Hispanic Black people. One caution belongs with that gradient rather than after it. CDC's own 2026 material notes greater bone density and fewer fractures in that group despite lower blood levels, and says bone outcomes do not always align with serum concentrations across population groups.

**Clinical prevalence.** The adult clinical endpoint is osteomalacia. **No reliable US prevalence exists**, because the survey measures the biomarker and not the disease. The nearest defensible proxy is the 5.0% below 30 nmol/L, which is a risk category and not a diagnosis.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical.** Evidence grade **insufficient**, with one honest qualification.

Observational evidence finds an association. A meta-analysis of 14 studies and 31,424 adults linked low blood levels to depression, confounded in the obvious directions by outdoor activity, sunlight, adiposity, immobility and general illness.

Randomized evidence is null, at scale. In 18,353 adults over a median 5.3 years, supplementation gave a hazard ratio of 0.97 for depression, and the authors concluded the findings do not support using vitamin D to prevent depression (Okereke 2020, [PMID 32749491](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32749491/)). In 20,487 older Australians, the difference in depression score was 0.02 ([PMID 36462182](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36462182/)). A meta-analysis of 9 trials in 4,923 people with depression or depressive symptoms found no reduction. Cognitive decline was null in the largest trial's ancillary study.

**The qualification matters and should be in the course.** These trials largely supplemented people who were already replete. In the largest, only 2.4% had baseline levels below 30 nmol/L. The National Institutes of Health states plainly that no study has evaluated whether supplementation helps people under care for clinical depression who have low levels and are taking antidepressants. That is exactly the population this course concerns. The honest cell reads **not studied**, rather than no effect.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical.** Evidence grade **insufficient**. Osteomalacia produces bone pain, proximal myopathy, muscle weakness and difficulty rising from a chair, all well established. No acceptable primary evidence attributes depression, psychosis or cognitive impairment to confirmed osteomalacia. There is a reasonable clinical observation that pain, weakness and fatigue can be misread as somatic depression, and it is worth a sentence, but no controlled data quantify it.

**Tests.** Serum total 25-hydroxyvitamin D. The assay is not a detail: method bias is documented, mass spectrometry is preferred, and the C3 epimer must be separated chromatographically or patients can be misclassified as deficient. **1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D is not the status test** and ordering it routinely is a recognized error, because it is tightly regulated and can be normal or high in deficiency.

**Risk factors.** Glucocorticoids, with deficiency in 11% of oral steroid users against 5% of non-users. Enzyme-inducing anticonvulsants, several of which are used as mood stabilizers. Orlistat. Age, skin pigmentation, latitude and season, limited sun exposure. Obesity, through sequestration in fat rather than impaired synthesis. Fat malabsorption and bariatric surgery. Note that thiazides are not a deficiency risk, and the hazard runs the other way toward hypercalcemia.

### Iron, including deficiency without anemia

**The cutoff drives the number more than the population does.** This belongs in the first line of the row.

| Definition | Prevalence | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Ferritin below 15 µg/L, inflammation-adjusted, women 12 to 49 | 13% in 2003 to 2004 rising to **18%** in 2021 to 2023 | CDC 2026 report |
| Ferritin below 15 µg/L, nonpregnant women 20 to 45 | 17.3% | Xiao 2026, [PMID 41926767](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41926767/), NHANES 1999 to 2023 |
| Transferrin saturation below 15%, women 20 to 44 | 24.7% | Williams 2025, [PMID 40324523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40324523/) |
| WHO 2020 adjusted criteria, women 18 to 49 | 27.4% | Singh 2026, [PMID 42662160](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42662160/) |
| Ferritin below 25 µg/L, females 12 to 21 | 38.6% | Weyand 2023, [PMID 37367984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37367984/) |
| Ferritin below 50 µg/L, females 12 to 21 | 77.5% | Weyand 2023, [PMID 37367984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37367984/) |
| Pregnancy | 47.0% by ferritin | Williams 2025, [PMID 40324523](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40324523/) |

**The single most useful fact in this row: among those with iron deficiency, 83.6% did not have anemia** (Weyand 2023, [PMID 37367984](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37367984/)). Screening on hemoglobin misses five in six.

**The conventional threshold is expert opinion, and a better one exists.** The World Health Organization's 15 µg/L was not derived from physiology, and the organization has said it lacked data to revise it. Federal researchers derived thresholds from the points where hemoglobin and soluble transferrin receptor actually start to move: **about 25 µg/L** in nonpregnant women, replicated in a second survey (Mei 2021, [PMID 34329578](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34329578/)). At that threshold prevalence roughly doubles.

**Clinical prevalence.** Iron deficiency anemia in nonpregnant women 20 to 45 rose from 4.3% in 1999 to 2000 to **9.1%** in 2021 to 2023. Note the direction of travel: subclinical deficiency was roughly flat across that period while anemia and iron deficiency anemia roughly doubled. The hemoglobin cutoff changes the answer by a factor of nearly three.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical.** Once the mechanism story is removed, as the brief instructs, the case is much thinner than the popular version.

*Restless legs syndrome.* Evidence grade **strong**, and it is randomized. A task force review of 31 studies found intravenous ferric carboxymaltose effective for moderate to severe restless legs, and oral iron possibly effective (Allen 2018, [PMID 29425576](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29425576/)). Two cautions. The ferritin thresholds at which iron works, below 300 and at or below 75, are far above any deficiency threshold, so this is not the same claim as restless legs being caused by subclinical deficiency. And it deserves the course's attention mainly because it is commonly misattributed and because several antidepressants and antipsychotics aggravate it.

*Fatigue.* Evidence grade **weak to moderate**. Self-reported fatigue improves, with a pooled effect of 0.38 standard deviations across 4 trials and 714 participants (Houston 2018, [PMID 29626044](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29626044/)). Objective physical capacity does not improve at all, across 9 trials. Compounding that dissociation, the larger positive trial was closed-label with only observer blinding, so participants knew their allocation (Vaucher 2012, [PMID 22777991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22777991/)). A subjective-only benefit in a partly unblinded trial is what expectancy produces. The right phrasing is a modest effect on how tired people say they feel, with no effect on what they can do.

*Mood.* Evidence grade **insufficient, trending negative**, and this is the direct answer. The best-matched trial repleted non-anemic iron-deficient menstruating women for 12 weeks, confirmed the biochemistry moved, and found **no effect on depression, anxiety or quality of life** (Vaucher 2012). No randomized trial shows that iron repletion improves depression or anxiety in non-anemic iron-deficient adults.

The cross-sectional evidence contradicts itself by sex, and on the sex that matters here. In a national sample of adults aged 18 to 25, ferritin deficiency was associated with higher depression risk in men, with a confidence interval spanning two orders of magnitude, and with **lower** depression risk in women ([PMID 38226328](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38226328/)). That is evidence against the popular claim, not for it.

*Cognition.* One small blinded trial found iron-sufficient women outperformed women with iron deficiency anemia at baseline, with iron-deficient non-anemic women intermediate, and improvement in ferritin tracked improvement in performance (Murray-Kolb 2007, [PMID 17344500](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17344500/)). Suggestive, single center, 113 completers, and the headline is a within-trial association rather than a clean between-group contrast.

*ADHD-like presentation.* Evidence grade **insufficient**. The randomized evidence is 23 children allocated 3 to 1, with within-group rather than between-group significance and non-significance on both Conners scales. It does not support a claim in children, let alone in adults.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical.** Weakness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating and impaired cognition are described in clinical accounts rather than demonstrated in trials. Pica and compulsive ice chewing are recognized behavioral features and can present as an odd compulsive symptom, which is worth knowing in a psychiatric setting. **No reliable evidence that correcting iron deficiency anemia in adults improves depression.**

**Tests.** Ferritin, and the threshold decides the answer. **Ferritin is an acute phase protein**, and inflammation was associated with 24.6% higher ferritin in national data, so it can mask deficiency. Three ways to handle that: raise the cutoff, exclude people with raised inflammatory markers, or regression-correct. Transferrin saturation below 15% had the best discrimination of the clinical indicators but overestimates prevalence. Soluble transferrin receptor has the property ferritin lacks, in that it is not strongly affected by inflammation, but assays differ and there is no generally accepted cutoff. Mean cell volume is specific and insensitive, at 32% sensitivity, so a normal value does not exclude deficiency.

**Risk factors.** Menstruation and heavy menstrual bleeding, which may account for a third to two fifths of iron deficiency anemia in reproductive-age women. Pregnancy. Blood donation, where a quarter to a third of regular donors become deficient. Vegetarian and vegan diets, for which the requirement is set 1.8 times higher because bioavailability is 5% to 12% rather than 14% to 18%. Obesity. Gastrointestinal disease and surgery. Cancer, where up to 60% of people with colon cancer are deficient at diagnosis, which is the reason occult blood loss must be excluded in older adults.

Two drug interactions run the other way and are worth listing because they affect the same patients. Iron reduces absorption of levodopa, which is used to treat restless legs, and of levothyroxine.

---

### Magnesium

**Belongs as a medication interaction row, not as a population deficiency row.** That is the conclusion, and the reasoning matters because magnesium is the nutrient with the most active promotion behind it.

**Intake inadequacy.** From federal dietary survey data, food and beverages only: 57% of men aged 19 and over and 52% of women fall below the estimated average requirement. Worst groups are men 19 to 30 at 64% and both sexes over 71 at about 60%. **On this nutrient, women are not worse off**, because their requirement is lower too. There is also a live objection to the requirement itself: pooling controlled metabolic ward balance studies suggests it should be roughly 175 to 250 mg a day rather than 255 to 350, which would mean the shortfall figure is inflated by construction ([PMID 27778151](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27778151/)).

**Biochemical deficiency: no good prevalence estimate exists, and the cell should say exactly that.** More than 99% of body magnesium sits inside cells and in bone, and under 1% is in serum, held under tight control. There is still no simple, accurate laboratory test of total body magnesium status.

**On the 67.8% figure now entering circulation**, see the corrections file. Short version: the threshold sits inside the reference interval the same paper derived, it originates in an argued opinion piece rather than a diagnostic study, no clinical laboratory or professional society has adopted it, the analysis was funded by a supplement manufacturer, and one author appears on the intake figure, the threshold and the serum figure alike. The paper also states that serum has limited diagnostic value, then uses serum to make the estimate.

**Clinical hypomagnesemia.** About 20% of hospitalized adults and 6% of ambulatory patients, both selection-biased upward because magnesium is not on a routine panel and gets ordered on suspicion. In community-dwelling adults with normal kidneys and no relevant drugs it is uncommon, because the kidney limits excretion. Symptoms generally appear only well below the usual cutoff.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical.** Evidence grade **insufficient**, and the pattern is the clearest demonstration of method effects in this whole table.

Pooling 21,927 dietary and 714 trial participants, the effect size depends almost entirely on the design: **uncontrolled trials give 1.60 standard deviations, placebo-controlled trials give 0.21** (Phelan 2018, [PMID 29897029](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29897029/)). Blood magnesium in people with mood disorders was *higher* than in controls. The prospective dietary association was not significant. The authors concluded there is little evidence for magnesium's involvement in mood disorders.

The single trial everyone cites has no placebo, no blinding of participants or investigators, self-reported outcomes collected by telephone by an unblinded team, and an effect larger than antidepressants achieve against placebo ([PMID 28654669](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28654669/)). Two features are decisive against it: **baseline magnesium was never measured**, so it cannot support a claim about correcting deficiency, and **the effect was the same regardless of baseline magnesium**, which is the opposite of what repletion predicts.

Observational associations attenuate by two thirds on adjustment, and the adjustments never include diet quality. Magnesium intake in a US population is largely a proxy for vegetables, legumes, nuts and whole grains.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical.** Evidence grade **weak**, and attribution is unsafe. Severe depletion impairs parathyroid hormone secretion and potassium conservation, so it arrives as a package with low calcium and low potassium, and tetany, seizures and delirium are classic features of low calcium in their own right. In the reported cases the three are almost never separable. Worth noting for this audience: **the Food and Drug Administration's own list of serious effects contains no psychiatric symptom.**

**Tests.** Serum magnesium is a poor measure, with the useful asymmetry that a low value establishes a real deficit while a normal one does not exclude depletion.

**RBC magnesium is not validated, and this is the direct answer to a question the course will face.** Tested against retention on intravenous loading in 103 intensive care patients and 41 healthy subjects, **erythrocyte and urinary magnesium did not predict retention at all**, and only total plasma magnesium did, weakly ([PMID 15816595](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15816595/)). It also barely moves on supplementation, rising 1.6% against plasma's 5.3%. It has one head-to-head test against a reference standard and it failed. No clinical decision should rest on it.

**Risk factors, which is where this row earns its place.** Proton pump inhibitors, especially beyond a year and alongside a diuretic. Loop and thiazide diuretics. Alcohol use disorder, where magnesium retention on loading was 33% against 3% to 4% in controls, the highest of any group tested. Type 2 diabetes, with the caveat that glycosuria causes magnesium wasting, so the direction is uncertain.

**Suggested cell text:** check magnesium in a symptomatic patient on a proton pump inhibitor for over a year, on a loop diuretic, with alcohol use disorder, or with diabetes. Do not screen the general population. Do not order red cell magnesium.

### Zinc

**Belongs only as an intake row, with an explicit flag that no valid status test exists.**

**Intake inadequacy.** 19% of men and 18% of women aged 19 and over below the estimated average requirement, rising to about 27% in both sexes over 71.

**Biochemical status is not meaningfully measurable in individuals**, and that is the expert panel's own position rather than an inference. Serum zinc is held constant across a wide range of intakes, the association with intake is poor at the individual level, diurnal variation and recent meals shift it, inflammation lowers it independently of status, and low albumin lowers it with no change in body zinc. The panel calls its own cutoffs statistical constructs rather than functionally derived thresholds, and restricts them to populations ([PMID 26962190](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26962190/)).

National data confirm it empirically. An afternoon blood draw shifts the value by 9.7 µg/dL and an evening draw by 15.3, against a cutoff separation of about 10. Low albumin raises the odds of a low reading elevenfold. And decisively: **zinc from diet or supplements did not affect serum zinc** in a nationally representative US sample ([PMID 29947812](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29947812/)). The biomarker does not track the exposure.

**Neuropsychiatric evidence.** Evidence grade **insufficient**. Depressed people do have lower serum zinc, by about 1.85 µmol/L across 17 studies, but with very high heterogeneity and a gradient that tracks illness acuity: larger in inpatients than in community samples. That is what reverse causation through the acute phase response predicts. Plasma zinc falls during inflammation, depression is associated with inflammation and lower albumin, and low albumin alone raises the odds of a low reading elevenfold.

The trial literature does not rescue it. Every dedicated placebo-controlled trial together randomized fewer than 250 people, the largest single trial had 60, and **every positive trial comes from Poland or Iran**. The one trial outside that cluster testing mood directly was negative. The meta-analyses contradict each other about whether the effect is in monotherapy or in add-on use, and one network analysis produces an effect roughly three times that of any licensed antidepressant, which is a marker of small-study bias rather than of efficacy.

**One safety note belongs here rather than in its own row.** High-dose zinc causes copper deficiency, and copper deficiency myeloneuropathy presents with gait instability and sensory disturbance. In a deprescribing setting that is exactly what gets misread as protracted withdrawal during a taper.

### Omega-3 (EPA and DHA)

**Recommendation: leave it out of the table.** Placing it in a nutrient deficiency table is a category error, and it is the row most likely to be misused.

**There is no estimated average requirement and no recommended allowance for EPA and DHA, so inadequacy is not formally definable.** The only frank essential fatty acid deficiency syndrome is an omega-6 condition of surgical and malabsorptive patients, diagnosed by a fatty acid ratio, and it is a non-issue in unselected adults.

**On the 98% figure.** A federal survey now exists and numerically supports it. CDC and the National Institutes of Health measured red cell membranes in 7,213 people and found **97.6% of adults below 8%**, with a population mean of 4.12% and **54% below 4%** ([PMID 42291144](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42291144/)). The authors declare no conflicts and no funding.

Three things change what that means. The 8% threshold is a **coronary heart disease** threshold, from a 2004 paper asking whether the index predicts death from coronary disease ([PMID 15208005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15208005/)). **It has never been validated against any psychiatric outcome.** When the commercial laboratory first circulated the 98% claim there was no probability sample behind it, only self-submitted samples, so the claim has been vindicated by accident rather than by the evidence available at the time. And "below optimal" does heavy rhetorical work: 97.6% below 8% and 54% below 4% describe the same distribution, and CDC chose to headline the second.

The conflict of interest should be stated wherever the index appears. Its originator and principal promoter founded and runs the commercial laboratory that sells the test.

**The trial evidence points the wrong way.** The Cochrane review of 35 studies found 0.40 standard deviations, described the effect as **unlikely to be clinically meaningful**, found no effect on remission, response or quality of life, and warned that the estimate **may be biased toward a positive finding** on funnel plot inspection and comparison with large well-conducted trials ([PMID 34817851](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34817851/)). Then the largest trial, 18,353 people over a median 5.3 years, found depression risk **significantly higher** in the supplemented arm, with a hazard ratio of 1.13 ([PMID 34932079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34932079/)). Its authors concluded the findings do not support using omega-3 to prevent depression.

If the brief asks for omega-3 in the course, it belongs in a section on dietary patterns and cardiovascular health, framed as intake and status rather than deficiency, with the depression evidence reported as null.

### Thiamine (B1)

**This is the most important row in the table**, and it is the one that most directly serves what the course is teaching. It is also the row where the popular literature is least involved, because nobody sells thiamine.

**A structural fact first.** NHANES has **never measured a thiamine biomarker**, in any cycle through August 2023. There is no US population prevalence of thiamine deficiency and there never has been. The National Institutes of Health states this directly. Anything presented as one is either an intake figure or a clinical sample.

**Intake, clearly labeled as intake:** 6% of the US population below the estimated average requirement.

**Measured status exists only in clinical samples**, all single-center: about 20% of emergency patients with sepsis or diabetic ketoacidosis with alcohol use disorder excluded, 26% of hospitalized veterans by plasma, 32% of a weight-management clinic population, 26% within a year of sleeve gastrectomy.

**Clinical prevalence, from autopsy.** Wernicke lesions were found in **1.1% to 2.8%** of sequential autopsies in Australian series, concentrated among people with a history of alcohol misuse.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, clinical.** Evidence grade **strong**, and the finding the course needs is not the symptom list but the miss rate.

**Eighty percent of necropsy-confirmed cases were never diagnosed during life.** Of 131 such cases, **only 16% had the classic triad** and **19% had no documented clinical signs at all** (Harper 1986, [PMID 3701343](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3701343/)). Independent series put the in-life diagnosis rate at 14% and 16%. The triad is not the presentation. It is the exception.

**What replaced the triad.** Operational criteria requiring **two of four**: dietary deficiency, eye movement abnormality, cerebellar dysfunction, or altered mental state or mild memory impairment (Caine 1997, [PMID 9010400](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9010400/)). Inter-rater reliability was complete across three raters. European neurological guidance adopted these.

Recovery is partial. Eye movement problems respond quickly. Nystagmus, ataxia, disturbed mental function and neuropathy respond incompletely. Untreated, up to 20% die, and survivors develop the amnestic syndrome, in which about a quarter do not recover even with treatment.

**Neuropsychiatric symptoms, subclinical.** Evidence grade **weak but not nothing**, and one trial is worth the course's attention. In 120 young women **whose thiamine status was adequate by the conventional criterion**, 50 mg a day for two months improved self-rated clearheadedness, composure and energy, and reaction times, with memory unchanged (Benton 1997, [PMID 9122365](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9122365/)). Self-reported outcomes, but it is the best evidence that a normal thiamine result does not exclude a thiamine-responsive state.

**Tests.** Whole blood or red cell thiamine diphosphate by chromatography, or the functional transketolase activation test, which is barely available anywhere. **Serum or plasma thiamine is a poor test** because most body thiamine is inside cells and phosphorylated, so plasma tracks the last meal.

One honest contradiction to carry: a 2026 study of 286 hospitalized veterans found the reverse of standard teaching, with low plasma in 26.4% against low whole blood in 2.7% of the same people, and most of the low-plasma group having clinical signs that improved on treatment. Single study, uncontrolled, and it should not be suppressed.

**Testing does not come first.** The European guidance is to draw the sample immediately before treating and then treat, with 200 mg three times daily, preferably intravenously, **before any carbohydrate**. Thiamine is very safe. Cochrane found only two eligible randomized trials totaling 177 people and concluded the evidence cannot guide dose, route or duration.

**Risk factors.** Alcohol use disorder above all, then bariatric surgery, hyperemesis in pregnancy, refeeding, chronic dialysis, AIDS, rapidly progressing blood cancers, and fluorouracil chemotherapy. In published hyperemesis cases the outcomes are severe: chronic cognitive disorder in 65%, pregnancy loss in 50%.

**One correction the course should carry.** The widely repeated figure that up to 80% of people with chronic alcohol use disorder are thiamine deficient traces to textbook chapters, not to a study with a stated biomarker and denominator. Two modern measurements contradict it. In 127 patients presenting in alcohol withdrawal, **99% were within or above the reference interval** ([PMID 31752901](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31752901/)), and in 100 consecutively admitted alcohol-dependent adults there was one case of deficiency and no Wernicke encephalopathy. The likely explanation is that routine supplementation already works. The case for giving thiamine empirically now rests on the autopsy evidence of missed disease, not on measured deficiency prevalence, and teaching it that way is more honest and just as actionable.

### Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine)

**Subclinical prevalence.** Serum pyridoxal phosphate was measured in **NHANES 2005 to 2010 only** and has not been measured since, so every current figure is at least sixteen years old. Deficiency below 20 nmol/L ran at 8% to 11% across those cycles, and insufficiency below 30 nmol/L at 22% to 26%. CDC's own caveat is worth repeating: that cutoff may overstate the requirement for more than half the group.

**The oral contraceptive claim, which the brief asked about specifically. Half of it holds and half does not.**

The biochemical half is real and large. Among current oral contraceptive users not taking supplements, **about three quarters had pyridoxal phosphate below 20 nmol/L**, and even at the highest intake category 39% remained below it (Morris 2008, [PMID 18469270](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18469270/)). That is the most striking subgroup figure in the US B6 literature.

**No symptom has been shown to follow from it**, and four separate lines say so.

The functional marker is normal. Despite the large difference in pyridoxal phosphate, homocysteine was the same in current users and in women who had never used hormones.

Repletion does not change function. Ten users given 10 mg daily for 28 days raised their pyridoxal phosphate more than fivefold with **no change in one-carbon metabolism** measured by labeled tracer ([PMID 26201817](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26201817/)).

The functional enzyme assay does not move. Across 55 women on seven preparations, pyridoxal phosphate fell in the first three months and **returned to normal by six**, while the functional index did not change at all ([PMID 2527729](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2527729/)).

The one adequately powered symptom trial was null. 124 women, 150 mg daily for 30 days, measuring nausea, headache, vomiting, dizziness, depression and irritability: **no significant difference on any symptom** ([PMID 9179457](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9179457/)). The one positive mood trial had eight participants and moved one of its two instruments.

**Suggested cell text:** a reproducible biochemical finding with no demonstrated clinical consequence, transient on modern low-dose preparations, with functional markers untouched.

**B6 for premenstrual symptoms.** Evidence grade **weak**, and the quality caveat has to travel with the result. A systematic review of 9 randomized placebo-controlled trials in 940 patients found an odds ratio of 2.32 for overall improvement and 1.69 for depressive symptoms (Wyatt 1999, [PMID 10334745](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10334745/)). The authors' own words: the overall methodology was poor, **none of the trials justified its sample size with a power calculation**, and the conclusions are limited by that. The current review reached the same verdict 26 years later, rating **only 1 of 31 trials at low risk of bias** ([PMID 38684926](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38684926/)). So the effect direction is consistent, the doses are 50 to 100 mg a day, and the entire evidence base is trials that would not pass contemporary review.

**One thing that is unambiguous, and belongs in the course.** Isoniazid causes B6-responsive peripheral neuropathy, established in controlled trials from 1960 where 19 of 20 cases occurred in the high-dose arms and a B-complex preparation prevented it entirely, attributable to just 6 mg of pyridoxine ([PMID 13722334](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13722334/)). Current guidance is 25 to 50 mg daily for anyone at risk, 100 mg for established neuropathy.

**A testing caveat with wide reach.** Inflammation lowers pyridoxal phosphate by about 28% independently of B6 intake or stores. Any low reading in an inflammatory condition inherits that confounder.

**Other risk factors.** Chronic dialysis, where roughly two thirds are low. Celiac disease and inflammatory bowel disease in remission. Carbidopa and levodopa, where the mechanism is chemical rather than nutritional. Valproate, carbamazepine and phenytoin.

### Niacin (B3)

**Subclinical status: no data exist, and no claim should be made.** Niacin status has never been measured in NHANES, in any cycle. Intake below the estimated average requirement is 1% of US adults. **No human study attributes any neuropsychiatric symptom to biochemical, non-pellagrous niacin deficiency.** The course should say that plainly, because the subclinical-niacin-and-mood claim has no evidentiary base at all.

**Clinical deficiency is where this row earns its place**, and it earns it for the same reason thiamine does.

**Pellagra presents without the rash, and gets missed.** Among 74 necropsies of people with chronic alcoholism, **20, or 27%, had neuropathologically diagnosed pellagra, and in none had the diagnosis been made during life, because most had no skin lesions** (Ishii 1981, [PMID 7229643](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7229643/)). In a modern series of 16 alcohol-dependent patients, **only 3 had the full triad** and 4 presented with a mental disorder as the chief complaint. In 22 heavy drinkers with autopsy-proven lesions identical to endemic pellagra, the features were confusion, marked oppositional rigidity and myoclonus, and the condition was often unrecognized because other alcohol-related encephalopathies coexisted (Serdaru 1988, [PMID 3401685](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3401685/)).

The psychiatric picture, from clinical description: depression, apathy, headache, fatigue, and memory loss that can progress to aggressive, paranoid and suicidal behavior, with auditory and visual hallucinations. Pellagra encephalopathy belongs in the differential for acute psychosis in the context of chronic alcohol use.

**The treatment trap, which is the most actionable finding in this section, with its caveat attached.** In that autopsy series, 15 of 22 patients received thiamine and pyridoxine without niacin, and this appeared to aggravate the neurological state or trigger pellagra encephalopathy in 9 cases.

**The caveat is that the companion pathology paper on the same 22 patients disagrees**, reporting no differences between cases given thiamine and pyridoxine and those not given them ([PMID 3401686](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3401686/)). The clinical claim and the pathological claim come from the same cohort and do not agree, so this should not be presented as settled.

What holds it up is independent corroboration from three other sources. A series of 18 pellagra patients reported that **two treated initially with thiamine alone, and one given inadequate niacin, deteriorated markedly in mental function and responded to niacin** ([PMID 864902](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/864902/)). A case of an alcohol-dependent patient given B-complex tablets without niacin who developed nervousness, insomnia, then delusions, hallucinations, loss of consciousness and myoclonus, all resolving fully on niacin ([PMID 15242145](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15242145/)). And a case in which thiamine was ineffective and symptoms improved only after nicotinamide.

The defensible version for the course: **treat suspected encephalopathy in an alcohol-dependent patient with multiple vitamins rather than thiamine alone.** That is low cost, low risk, and supported whether or not the precipitation effect is real.

A second reason not to rely on the eight-decade-old framing: one modern series of 16 alcohol-dependent patients with pellagra found two also had Wernicke encephalopathy and three were in alcohol withdrawal at presentation. The syndromes coexist, which is part of why each gets missed.

**A second setting worth naming.** In newly diagnosed carcinoid syndrome, 10 of 36 patients were biochemically niacin deficient and **only 1 was clinically diagnosed with pellagra**. The clinical diagnosis is specific and badly insensitive.

**Isoniazid again.** In a matched case-control study of 197 pellagra cases, isoniazid exposure carried an adjusted odds ratio of **42.6** ([PMID 35427527](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35427527/)). Not a US population, but it is the only controlled quantification that exists, and isoniazid is a structural analog of niacin.

**Tests.** There is no good one. Blood niacin levels are not reliable indicators and no functional test reflects total body stores. The most sensitive available measure is a 24-hour urine collection for two metabolites, with cutoffs from a 1998 committee report. The two research indices in circulation rest on studies of 7 and 92 people. Pellagra is a clinical diagnosis confirmed by response to treatment.

## Nutrients considered and excluded, with reasons

The brief asks directly which of the circulating candidates produce neuropsychiatric symptoms in adults, and said that a well-sourced exclusion was as useful as an inclusion. These are the exclusions established so far.

### Vitamin E: excluded

The circulating figure of 80% to 88% of adults not getting enough is intake below an estimated average requirement. Measured status tells a different story: under 1% of US adults are biochemically inadequate by serum alpha-tocopherol below 11.6 µmol/L ([PMID 23596164](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23596164/)). Two orders of magnitude, from the same survey program in overlapping years.

Independently of that, frank vitamin E deficiency in US adults is confined to fat malabsorption states and a rare inherited ataxia, and its presentation is neurological rather than psychiatric. It cannot support a row in an introductory table either way.

### Potassium: excluded

Potassium has an adequate intake, not an estimated average requirement. An adequate intake is set from observed intake in apparently healthy people when the evidence is too thin to establish a requirement, so near-universal shortfall against it describes the threshold rather than the population. It is not a deficiency prevalence.

### Vitamin C: excluded from the general-population claim

The biochemical data is good and it does not support a general-population concern. Deficiency was 7.1% in 2003 to 2004 ([PMID 19675106](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19675106/)) and 6.8% in 2017 to 2018 ([PMID 36592081](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36592081/)), statistically unchanged.

Two details are worth carrying into the course even though the nutrient is excluded. **Women have higher serum vitamin C than men**, by roughly 10 µmol/L, so the instruction to prioritize findings in women does not apply here. The risk gradient runs on smoking, low income and obesity. And fatigue, the symptom usually offered for this nutrient, overlaps with the diagnostic criteria for depression, which is a reason for caution about attributing it to a nutrient rather than a reason to count it as a psychiatric symptom.

Frank scurvy in identifiable at-risk groups is a different matter and may warrant a line in the course text rather than a row in this table.

### Calcium: excluded

Two unrelated things are being merged. **Dietary calcium inadequacy causes bone loss, not psychiatric symptoms.** In a person with working parathyroid glands, low dietary calcium is compensated by hormone-driven bone resorption and blood calcium stays normal. The cost is skeletal.

**Low blood calcium does produce neuropsychiatric features**, including anxiety, irritability, depression and cognitive impairment. Its causes are hypoparathyroidism, post-surgical states, severe vitamin D deficiency, low magnesium, kidney failure and pancreatitis. **Not diet.**

Serum calcium is a test for a parathyroid, kidney or vitamin D problem. It is not a test of calcium nutrition. If the course wants the clinical entity, it belongs in a low-calcium row under electrolyte abnormalities with diet explicitly excluded as a cause.

### Copper: excluded as a row, kept as a footnote to zinc

Across 75 patients in three case series, **the presenting complaint was gait, in every case** ([PMID 15249607](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15249607/)). The syndrome is dorsal column sensory ataxia with spasticity and neuropathy, plus anemia and low white cells. No mood, psychotic or primary cognitive feature appears in the series. There is no prevalence figure to put in a cell, and where frank deficiency occurs in adults it is surgical or iatrogenic.

It also invites a specific confusion. Most literature linking copper to psychiatric symptoms concerns copper **excess** in Wilson disease, where psychiatric presentation is common and misdiagnosis as schizophrenia is documented.

The footnote it earns is a safety one. High-dose zinc causes copper deficiency, and the resulting gait instability and sensory disturbance are exactly what gets misread as protracted withdrawal during a taper.

### Iodine: excluded

The chain runs from iodine deficiency to hypothyroidism to psychiatric symptoms, and it is real. But **the clinical action at every point on that chain is to check thyroid function.** A patient with neuropsychiatric symptoms from iodine deficiency has hypothyroidism, which a thyroid test detects. A patient with normal thyroid function does not have neuropsychiatric symptoms from iodine whatever their iodine level. Urinary iodine is a population indicator and cannot diagnose an individual.

Two findings carry forward regardless. Median urinary iodine in US women of reproductive age fell from 133 to 101 µg/L between 2001 and 2018 ([PMID 42163862](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42163862/)), with non-Hispanic Asian women lowest at 81 ([PMID 29986412](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29986412/)). And **lithium** inhibits thyroid hormone release and causes hypothyroidism and goiter, while amiodarone does both. That is a thyroid row for a deprescribing course, and lithium makes it directly relevant.

### Omega-3: excluded

Full reasoning in the omega-3 section above. In short: there is no requirement, no deficiency state and no deficiency syndrome to have, the 8% threshold is a coronary heart disease threshold ([PMID 15208005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15208005/)) never validated for any psychiatric outcome, and the largest randomized trial found depression risk **higher** in the supplemented arm ([PMID 34932079](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34932079/)).

## Gaps worth telling the brief about

Four absences came up repeatedly, and naming them is more useful than filling them.

**Nobody has run the trial this table is about.** No study has randomized adults **selected for measured low status** of any of these nutrients to repletion and measured neuropsychiatric outcomes. Every trial in the literature selects on the psychiatric diagnosis instead. That is the structural defect behind the null results for folate, B12, vitamin D, magnesium and zinc, and it is not fixable by adding more of the same studies.

**No trial has tested whether stopping a drug reverses a neuropsychiatric outcome.** For a deprescribing course this is the question that matters most, and the randomized evidence does not exist for any of the drug and nutrient pairs here.

**The one population the course cares about has never been studied for vitamin D.** No study has evaluated whether supplementation helps people under care for clinical depression who have low blood levels and are taking antidepressants. The large null trials supplemented people who were already replete.

**Two nutrients have never been measured in the national survey.** Thiamine and niacin status have never appeared in any cycle. Serum B12 was dropped after 2013 to 2014, and pyridoxal phosphate after 2010. So for four of the nutrients in this table, there is no current national picture at all.

## What this table does not cover

Pain, which appears in the wider topic list but is outside the brief's specification. Dosing and supplement recommendations of any kind. Transdermal and topical supplementation, which is assessed separately. Any clinical instruction about what to do with a result.
